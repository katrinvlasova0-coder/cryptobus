## Start with policy, not a new wallet app

Corporate crypto treasury is a control system: who can hold balances, who can convert, who can pay, and what evidence closes the books. Tools come second. If policy is vague, every payable becomes an exception ticket.

## Separate roles for custody, conversion, and payables

A workable minimum:

1. Treasury owner sets buffers and conversion rules.
2. Payments ops executes approved payables.
3. Compliance owns screening exceptions.
4. Accounting owns recognition and evidence standards.
5. An executive sponsor owns incident escalation.

| Function | Typical control | Failure mode if missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Custody access | Dual control / hardware policy | Single-person compromise |
| Conversion | Quote window + limit | Surprise slippage |
| Payables | Maker-checker | Wrong address payout |
| Reporting | Daily position pack | Blind overnight risk |

## Conversion windows that match payable calendars

Buy crypto for supplier payouts against a calendar, not vibes. Align conversion with invoice due batches, OTC minimums, and bank funding cutoffs. For larger sizes, compare exchange vs [OTC desk usage](/blog/when-businesses-use-otc-crypto-desks/).

## Liquidity buffers without guesswork

Size buffers from: average weekly crypto payable volume, max single invoice, chain congestion contingency, and fiat top-up lag. Review monthly. A buffer is an ops reserve, not an investment thesis.

## Risks and limits

Balances can move with market prices when you hold non-stable assets. Operational mistakes (wrong asset, wrong network) can be irreversible. Compliance restrictions can block corridors even when liquidity exists.

## Checklist to stand up treasury controls

1. Write wallet and role policy.
2. Define conversion authority limits.
3. Set payable maker-checker rules.
4. Publish evidence standards for accounting.
5. Run a tabletop incident drill (wrong address / wrong asset).
6. Review buffers monthly.
