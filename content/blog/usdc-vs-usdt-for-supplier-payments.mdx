Choosing between USDC vs USDT for business payments usually comes down to four practical factors: issuer transparency, corridor liquidity, supplier preference, and reconciliation ease — not brand loyalty. Both are US-dollar-referenced stablecoins used to settle supplier invoices, but they come from different issuers with different disclosure practices, and their availability differs by exchange, network, and region. If you're setting a payment policy for accounts payable, the practical question isn't "which one is better" in the abstract — it's which one your supplier can receive, which corridor has deeper liquidity for your invoice size, and which documentation trail satisfies your internal controls. This comparison walks through what treasury and procurement teams typically check before adding either stablecoin to a payment run, without predicting price behavior or promising any settlement outcome.

## What USDC and USDT actually are

USDC (USD Coin) is issued by Circle, a US-based financial technology company that publishes monthly reserve attestations conducted by an independent accounting firm. USDT (Tether) is issued by Tether Limited, which has also increased the frequency and detail of its reserve reporting in recent years. Both tokens are designed to maintain a stable reference value to the US dollar, and both operate across multiple blockchain networks, including Ethereum, Tron, and others depending on the version issued.

For invoice settlement purposes, the technical distinction that matters most day-to-day is not the peg mechanism itself but:

1. Which networks each token supports for the corridor you're paying into
2. What attestation or reserve reporting your compliance team requires before approving a stablecoin for AP use
3. Whether your exchange or OTC desk offers competitive pricing and reasonable settlement speed for the pair
4. Whether the receiving supplier's wallet or exchange account actually supports that specific stablecoin and network
5. How your internal ledger and audit trail record the transaction for period-end reconciliation

Guidance from bodies such as the FATF (https://www.fatf-gafi.org) on virtual asset service providers, and evolving EU MiCA rules for asset-referenced and e-money tokens, apply to stablecoin issuers and the exchanges that list them — reviewing current regulatory status for both tokens in your jurisdiction is part of standard vendor onboarding, not a one-time check.

## How supplier preference shapes the decision

In practice, many businesses don't choose a stablecoin in isolation — the supplier's receiving capability often decides it. A supplier operating primarily in Southeast Asia or Latin America may have deeper existing USDT liquidity through local exchanges, while a supplier in the EU or US may prefer USDC because of Circle's more visible institutional relationships and reporting cadence. Before finalizing invoice currency terms, ask your supplier directly:

1. Which stablecoins does your business currently hold or regularly convert?
2. Which network (e.g., Ethereum mainnet versus a lower-fee alternative) do you prefer for receiving funds?
3. Do you have any internal policy restricting acceptance of a particular issuer?
4. What confirmation or reconciliation format do you need from us for your own bookkeeping?
5. Are there minimum or maximum transaction sizes your exchange or custody provider imposes?

This kind of scoping conversation reduces failed transfers and mismatched expectations, and it's a natural extension of setting up [multi-currency crypto invoice settlement](/blog/multi-currency-invoice-settlement-with-crypto/) processes where multiple stablecoins and fiat currencies coexist on the same AP calendar.

## Liquidity, spreads, and OTC considerations

For invoice amounts large enough to move exchange order books — commonly six figures and above in USD-equivalent terms — spread and slippage become a bigger practical concern than which stablecoin brand you use. This is where many finance teams evaluate [when businesses use OTC crypto desks](/blog/when-businesses-use-otc-crypto-desks/) instead of routing large conversions through retail exchange order books. An OTC desk can typically quote firm pricing for either USDC or USDT conversion, and comparing quotes side by side for the same notional amount is a more reliable liquidity check than general market commentary.

Cross-border corridors also matter. A corridor with deep USDT liquidity on local exchanges might settle faster and cheaper than routing the same payment through USDC if your counterparty bank or exchange doesn't actively support USDC pairs in that market. Reviewing corridor-specific behavior is closely related to broader questions covered in [cross-border crypto settlement vs bank wires](/blog/cross-border-crypto-settlement-vs-bank-wires/), where settlement speed and cost trade-offs are compared more generally.

## Reconciliation and accounting treatment

From a bookkeeping standpoint, USDC and USDT are generally treated similarly under most current accounting frameworks — both typically require judgment about classification (e.g., as a financial asset or intangible asset, depending on jurisdiction and facts) rather than being assigned a bespoke stablecoin category. The IFRS Foundation's ongoing digital assets materials (https://www.ifrs.org) provide background on how standard-setters are approaching crypto-asset classification generally, though specific tax and accounting guidance should come from your own advisor given your jurisdiction and reporting framework.

Practical reconciliation steps that apply regardless of which stablecoin you choose:

1. Record the invoice in its original fiat-denominated currency and log the stablecoin conversion rate at time of settlement
2. Retain the exchange or OTC desk trade confirmation alongside the wallet transaction hash
3. Capture network fees separately from the principal amount for expense categorization
4. Reconcile the supplier's confirmation of receipt against your ledger entry before closing the invoice
5. Flag any material timing gap between invoice date and settlement date for FX/rate documentation

Teams that already have a documented process for [pay business invoices with USDT](/blog/how-to-pay-business-invoices-with-usdt/) can generally extend the same workflow to USDC with minor adjustments to the wallet address format and network fee assumptions.

## Risks and limits to weigh before standardizing on one stablecoin

No stablecoin is free of operational, counterparty, or market risk, and neither USDC nor USDT should be treated as a risk-free settlement instrument. Before setting a company-wide policy, weigh the following:

- **Peg deviation risk.** Stablecoins are designed to track the US dollar closely but have historically shown temporary deviations during periods of market stress; a payment initiated and settled within a short window generally carries limited exposure, but this is not guaranteed.
- **Issuer counterparty risk.** Holding either token exposes you indirectly to the issuer's reserve management and operational solvency; reviewing current attestation reports is a reasonable ongoing practice rather than a one-time check.
- **Regulatory change.** Rules under frameworks like MiCA in the EU, and evolving guidance referenced by the FATF and national regulators, can affect which stablecoins exchanges and banks are willing to support in a given jurisdiction.
- **Network and technical risk.** Sending to an unsupported network or an incorrect contract address can result in unrecoverable loss of funds; always confirm the supplier's exact receiving network before transferring.
- **Liquidity concentration.** Thin liquidity for a given stablecoin-network combination in a specific corridor can widen spreads or delay settlement at the exact moment you need speed.

None of this should be read as a prediction of future price stability or issuer performance — it's an operational risk map to build controls around, consistent with the KYB and AML process reviews described in [corporate crypto treasury basics](/blog/corporate-crypto-treasury-basics/).

## Practical checklist for choosing between USDC and USDT

Use this as a working checklist when setting or revisiting your stablecoin payment policy:

1. Confirm which stablecoins your top suppliers already hold or accept without additional conversion steps
2. Check current attestation reports for both Circle and Tether before large-value payment runs
3. Compare live spreads on your exchange or OTC desk for the specific corridor and amount
4. Verify network compatibility on both the sending and receiving side before every first-time transfer
5. Document the chosen stablecoin, network, and rate on the invoice record for audit purposes
6. Review your jurisdiction's current regulatory treatment of each stablecoin, since this can change
7. Revisit the policy periodically rather than treating the initial choice as permanent

## Setting your policy without overcommitting

Many finance teams land on a dual-support policy: accept and pay in either USDC or USDT depending on supplier preference and corridor liquidity, rather than mandating one exclusively. This keeps procurement flexible while still requiring the same documentation, KYB, and reconciliation standard regardless of which stablecoin is used for a given invoice.
